Reading Luke · 3 · Luke 1:5–38

Greetings, Favored One!

On two announcements, a world that ran on honor and shame, and the kind of honor only God gives: a reading of Luke 1:5–38.

Young Kang·Hana Church·October 11, 2026·8 min read

The passage · Luke 1:5–7, 18–22, 26–30, 38

5In the days of King Herod of Judea, there was a priest named Zechariah, who belonged to the priestly order of Abijah. His wife was descended from the daughters of Aaron, and her name was Elizabeth.

6Both of them were righteous before God, living blamelessly according to all the commandments and regulations of the Lord.

7But they had no children because Elizabeth was barren, and both were getting on in years.

18Zechariah said to the angel, “How can I know that this will happen? For I am an old man, and my wife is getting on in years.”

19The angel replied, “I am Gabriel. I stand in the presence of God, and I have been sent to speak to you and to bring you this good news.

20But now, because you did not believe my words, which will be fulfilled in their time, you will become mute, unable to speak, until the day these things occur.”

21Meanwhile the people were waiting for Zechariah and wondering at his delay in the sanctuary.

22When he did come out, he was unable to speak to them, and they realized that he had seen a vision in the sanctuary. He kept motioning to them and remained unable to speak.

26In the sixth month the angel Gabriel was sent by God to a town in Galilee called Nazareth,

27to a virgin engaged to a man whose name was Joseph, of the house of David. The virgin’s name was Mary.

28And he came to her and said, “Greetings, favored one! The Lord is with you.”

29But she was much perplexed by his words and pondered what sort of greeting this might be.

30The angel said to her, “Do not be afraid, Mary, for you have found favor with God.”

38Then Mary said, “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word.” Then the angel departed from her.

Luke begins his story with two births announced by the same angel, six months apart. First Gabriel comes to an old priest named Zechariah in the temple in Jerusalem. Then he goes to a woman named Mary in a small town in Galilee. Each is told that a son will be born. Each is told not to be afraid. Each asks the angel a question.

The two scenes are built to be read together, and the differences between them are where Luke does his talking. Most of us know these stories well enough that we stop seeing them. Christmas pageants have smoothed them into one soft picture: an angel, a surprised listener, good news. But set the two side by side and read slowly, and something sharp comes into view. It has to do with a word that mattered more than almost anything in Luke’s world: honor.

Read down each column. One fills up with place, people, family, office and reputation. The other stays nearly blank until the row where the angel speaks. Why that matters is easier to see once we know how Luke’s world measured a person.

A world of honor and shame

In Luke’s world, Jewish, Greek and Roman alike, what people valued most was honor: your worth as other people saw it. Honor was public. It lived in other people’s eyes, and it was lost in front of them. That loss was shame.

Some honor you were born with: your family, your father’s name, your town, an office handed down to you. Some you won or lost in public, by what you did and how people judged it. That is why people in the Bible are so often introduced by their ancestry and their hometown. Those lines are not decoration. They are a person’s standing.

Zechariah had a great deal of the first kind. You did not apply to be a priest; you were born one, from the line of Aaron. Elizabeth came from Aaron’s line too. Their names carried a lineage on both sides.

But one thing could undo much of it. In that world children carried the family name, inherited the land and cared for their parents in old age. People believed children were a sign of God’s blessing, so they believed the reverse as well: if you had none, God must be displeased with you. And when a couple had no children, the blame almost always fell on the wife. The Bible knows this pain well, in Sarah, in Rachel, in Hannah. Luke says that Zechariah and Elizabeth had no children “because Elizabeth was barren” (1:7). Later the angel calls her the one “who was said to be barren” (1:36). That was what people called her. When a son is finally on the way, it is Elizabeth, not Zechariah, who speaks of “the disgrace I have endured among my people” (1:25), words that echo Rachel’s after her own long wait (Genesis 30:23). Yet Luke has already told us that both of them were “righteous before God” (1:6). Honor and shame did not track goodness.

Mary’s standing would have come, as a woman’s usually did, through the men in her life: first her father, then her husband. Luke names neither her father nor her family. He introduces her only through the man she is engaged to. And her town is small enough that he has to introduce it too: “a town in Galilee called Nazareth.” The Old Testament never mentions it once.

Silence at the height of honor

Now look again at Zechariah’s day. His priestly group is on duty, and the lot falls to him to go into the sanctuary and burn incense, as close to the presence of God as an ordinary priest would ever come. For most priests that came once in a lifetime, if it came at all.

And it was public. It was the hour of prayer, and “the whole assembly of the people was praying outside” (1:10). When the priest came back out, the crowd expected him to pronounce the priestly blessing over them. This was the most honored moment of Zechariah’s life, with everyone watching.

But when he finally comes out, he cannot speak. He keeps motioning to them, and they work out that he has seen a vision (1:21–22). The blessing they waited for never comes. At the height of his honor, in front of everyone, the priest cannot say a word, and the people go home.

Six months later the same angel goes to a town that has to be introduced, to a woman whose family is never named. There is no crowd, no altar, no office. She is “much perplexed” and asks her own question: “How can this be, since I am a virgin?” (1:34). And at the end of the scene, the one who speaks is her: “Here am I, the servant of the Lord; let it be with me according to your word” (1:38).

The man with all the honor goes silent in front of everyone. The woman with none is the one who speaks.

The honor God gives

Look at the greeting row again. “Greetings, favored one!” Nobody inherited that, and nobody earned it. Luke’s word for “favored” is built on the Greek word for grace. Mary is favored because God has favored her, and the angel says so twice: “you have found favor with God” (1:30).

So from its first pages Luke’s gospel turns honor and shame upside down. This is good news for the lowly, right from the start. But notice how God does it. He does not fix Mary’s column by handing her what Zechariah had: a better family tree, a position, a title. He gives honor another way.

Think about how the two sons’ lives turn out. John will live in the wilderness and die in a prison. Jesus will die on a cross, the most shameful death Rome had. By the world’s measure, neither story ends in honor. Yet the church confesses Jesus as Lord and remembers John as the one who prepared his way. No lineage could have given them that.

And even the world’s own measure was turned over. Luke names the emperors himself: Augustus when Jesus was born (2:1), Tiberius when John began to preach (3:1). For two thousand years the stories of a preacher from the wilderness and a crucified man from Nazareth have been read, sung and honored more than the story of any emperor. Few people today could tell you one thing Tiberius did. And the parents are remembered for one thing: they raised these two.

None of this makes Zechariah the villain. The reversal in Luke does not throw out the high so the low can take their seats. Elizabeth’s disgrace is taken away: the Lord “looked favorably on me,” she says (1:25). And when her son is born and the neighbors want to name him after his father, Zechariah asks for a writing tablet and writes, “His name is John.” Then “immediately his mouth was opened and his tongue freed, and he began to speak, praising God” (1:63–64). The priest who could not bless the people gets his voice back, and the first thing he does with it is praise God. God’s reversal has room for him too.

Honor now

What counts as honor today? It isn’t hard to list. Which school you graduated from. What job you landed. How much you make. What house you bought, what car you drive. How your family looks to the people around you. We may not use the word honor, but we know exactly which column we would like our lives to fill, and we feel the blank rows.

Luke does not answer by promising to fill them. He shows God giving honor another way, and he invites us to live by it: to live for other people, and to keep asking God to help us do it. In the world’s eyes that is a losing deal. You give more than you get back. In Korean there is a word for that person, hogu: the one who always seems to come out behind. I don’t mean letting people use you; no one should be told to put up with that. I mean the life you choose, freely, when you give to someone who cannot pay you back (14:13–14).

Yet that is where people keep finding what their lives mean, and where hope and peace come from. And there is something you only learn by living it: when you help someone else live, you come alive yourself.

So here is a question to carry. Look back over your life at the places the world would call failure: the school that said no, the job that went to someone else, the years that went sideways. What did God give you there? Mary’s column was nearly empty until God spoke. Some of the best things we have were given to us in rows we thought were blank.
